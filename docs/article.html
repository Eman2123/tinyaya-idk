Teaching TinyAya to say "I don't know" in languages with no labelled QA data

Code Adapters Data and outputs Results explorer
The short version
  • A small multilingual model (tiny-aya-global) answers every question it is asked and gets 80 to 85 percent of them wrong on our test set.
  • We taught it to say "I don't know" using known/unknown labels that need no gold answers in the target language.
  • That cut wrong answers to 19 to 26 percent in English, Spanish, Arabic and Malay, and matched training with gold labels in three of the four languages.
  • The price is real: the tuned model refuses many questions it could have answered.

A model that never says "I don't know"

Ask a small language model something it has no way of knowing, and it will usually answer anyway, confidently. We measured how often. On 252 held-out questions per language from MKQA, CohereLabs/tiny-aya-global answered every question and was wrong on 80 to 85 percent of all of them, in English, Spanish, Arabic and Malay.

The obvious fix is to fine-tune the model to abstain when it does not know. The hard part is the labels. To train abstention, you first need to know which questions the model does not know. Normally that means checking its answers against gold answers, and most languages do not have those.

So the question we asked is this: can we get those known/unknown labels without gold answers in the target language, and still end up with a model that abstains about as well as one trained on gold labels?

Two ways to guess what the model knows

We tried two gold-free labels.

Consistency. Sample five answers to the same question. If at least four agree, we call the question known. If the model keeps changing its mind, we call it unknown.

A hidden-state probe. We train a small logistic regression on a middle layer of the model (layer -12), using English questions where we do have gold answers. Then we apply that probe, unchanged, to Spanish, Arabic and Malay. English is the only language that ever sees a gold label.

Known questions keep their answer as the training target. Unknown questions get "I don't know" in the question's own language. We then fine-tune one small LoRA adapter (r=16) per language and compare five recipes:

RecipeWhere the known/unknown labels come from
R1Trap questions written by a teacher model
R2Gold answers (our upper bound)
R3Consistency
R4The transferred probe
R5Probe and consistency must agree (an ablation)

Everything ran on a Kaggle T4. In total that is 61 evaluation runs: the base model, five recipes, four languages and three seeds. We compare recipes with a paired bootstrap over the test questions.

What we found

Gold-free labels work. R4 matches gold-label training (R2) in English, Spanish and Malay. In each of those the bootstrap interval for the difference includes zero, so we cannot tell them apart.

Arabic is the exception. R4 is wrong 0.040 less often than R2, but it also gets 0.021 fewer answers right, because it abstains more. That is a more cautious model, not a better one.

Teacher-written traps barely help. R1 only moves wrong answers from about 0.8 to about 0.7 in English, Spanish and Malay. One possible reason is that the questions a teacher writes as traps are not the ones this small model actually gets wrong. We did not test that.

The probe is at least as good as consistency. To separate known from unknown questions, the probe reaches AUROC 0.84 to 0.90 and consistency 0.74 to 0.81. The intervals overlap, so we say "comparable or better", not "clearly better".

Combining both can help. R5, which keeps only questions where the probe and consistency agree, is wrong less often than R4 in Spanish and Malay. It refuses more as well.

The cost: refusing too much

Take the questions the base model answered correctly. R4 refuses between 19 and 38 percent of them depending on the language (19 to 49 percent across R2 to R5). If you care about being useful, that is the number to watch, and it is the main thing we would improve next.

Does it carry over to another language?

Only partly. An adapter trained on English also helps on Spanish and Malay, roughly 0.35 less hallucination than the base model, but much less on Arabic. Adapters trained on one non-English language transfer poorly to the others. This comes from a single seed, so treat it as a hint.

The Lexsi tools

We used two of them. CuratorKIT processed all 20 training sets (five recipes by four languages) and produced a manifest, provenance, checksums and a dataset card for each. It rejected no examples. AuditKIT re-ran our evaluation through its own scorers; on R4 Spanish, seed 1, it reported correct 0.119, refused 0.694 and hallucinated 0.1865, matching our own script. AlignTune did not install next to our transformers and trl versions, so we left it out.

What went wrong along the way

Our first evaluations were wrong. The tuned adapters did not stop generating at the end-of-sequence token, so they kept writing after the answer and exact match failed. For a while Arabic and Malay looked worse than the base model, and it was purely an evaluation bug. We fixed generation to stop at EOS and re-ran every evaluation except the base model.

We also lost about five hours of work when a Kaggle session reset wiped the working directory. After that, every finished run uploaded its adapter and results to the Hub straight away.

What these numbers do not show

  • One QA source (MKQA, translated questions) and small training sets of 238 to 382 examples.
  • The probe trains on English gold labels, so the method is gold-free only in the target languages.
  • The refusal templates and few-shot prompts for Spanish, Arabic and Malay were not checked by a native speaker.
  • Our safety check uses 8 English prompts and found no actionable harmful answers. It is a sanity check, not a safety evaluation.
  • The transfer matrix is one seed. The published adapters are seed 1 only; the raw outputs for all seeds are in the data collection.
  • The adapters inherit the non-commercial licence of the base model (CC BY-NC 4.0). The data follows MKQA (CC BY-SA 3.0).

Try it

from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM
from peft import PeftModel

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("CohereLabs/tiny-aya-global")
base = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
    "CohereLabs/tiny-aya-global", torch_dtype="float16", device_map="auto")
model = PeftModel.from_pretrained(
    base, "EmanMirza2025/tinyaya-idk-adapters", subfolder="R4_es_s1")

Adapter folders are named R{recipe}_{language}_s{seed}. Use the few-shot prompt from configs/config.yaml in the GitHub repository, because the reported numbers were measured with it. The repository also has a small Gradio demo (src/app.py) that shows the base model and an adapter side by side, and full reproduction steps in the README.
